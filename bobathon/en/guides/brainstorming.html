Idea guide · Start with your own work

When you are not sure what to automate

Start with work you repeat, tasks that take too long, or things you often miss-not with a grand idea.
A conversation with Bob can help you find the solution you actually need.

Good ideas start with work that has genuinely frustrated you

  • Think about moments in the past week when you repeated a task or had to wait.
  • Describe a specific situation you experienced rather than a generic problem.
  • Do not follow every prompt in order. Start with the one that is easiest to answer right now.

Reflect on your work

If you have not identified a frustrating task yet, let Bob ask questions to help you find one.

I want to find something in my work that would be useful to automate.
Ask me one question at a time so I can recall work I repeat, work that takes too long,
things I often miss, or information I copy between tools.
After you have heard enough, summarize the best candidates for automation.

Start with a frustrating work situation

A small frustration can be a strong automation candidate when it happens often.

At work, I felt frustrated when I had to [task] [when/where].
I currently handle it by [current process], and it usually takes or happens [time or frequency].
Ask questions and help me investigate the real problem that needs to be solved.
Do not jump to a solution. First, map the current workflow.

Turn repetitive work into an automation

Separating inputs, decisions, and outputs makes the automatable parts easier to see.

The task I repeat is "[describe the task]."
Break the workflow into its trigger, required inputs, decisions I make,
and final output.
Separate the parts Bob or an MCP could automate from the parts a person should review.

Explore several kinds of solutions

Automation does not need to replace the entire process. Explore smaller forms of help such as alerts, organization, drafts, and connections.

The current idea is "[one-sentence idea]."
Identify three assumptions behind this idea and reverse them.
Suggest different approaches from the perspectives of full automation, partial assistance,
error prevention, information summarization, and tool connection.

Narrow down the directions you like

Choose an idea that saves real time or prevents mistakes and can be demonstrated during the hackathon-not simply the most impressive idea.

Put the ideas so far into a table.
Compare each idea by time saved, mistakes prevented, frequency of use, implementation difficulty, and hackathon demo scenario.
Do not choose for me. Show the tradeoffs and questions I should answer so I can decide.

Keep only the essential capability

Once you choose an idea, reduce it to one core experience rather than every possible feature.

The selected idea is "[one-sentence idea]."
Reduce it to the smallest demo (MVP) that can show its core value within the hackathon.
List the must-have capability, what to cut, and the user demo flow.
Do not write code yet.
Next step: You are ready to build when you can say, "I am changing the repeated task ○○ so Bob can △△, reducing time and mistakes."

When the conversation stalls

  • "Make this direction more practical."
  • "Consider it from the completely opposite perspective."
  • "Ask me questions and help me find it."
  • "Do not evaluate yet; generate more candidates."
  • "Show me how to test it in 10 minutes."

You do not need to write the perfect prompt at once. The heart of brainstorming is adjusting the direction together with Bob.
